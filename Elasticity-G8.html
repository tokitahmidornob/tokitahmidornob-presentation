The Core Concept

Simple Explanation: The Law of Demand tells us which way things go: if the price goes up, people buy less. But for businesses, just knowing the direction isn't enough. They need to know how much less people will buy. Elasticity tells us the exact size of that change. It measures how sensitive people are to price changes.

Speaker 1: Toki Tahmid

Price Elasticity & Midpoint Method

Simple Explanation: If you use normal percentage math, you get different answers depending on whether the price is going up or down. That's confusing. The Midpoint Method fixes this problem by dividing the change by the average of the starting and ending numbers. This gives us one perfectly consistent answer every time.

Basic Formula

E = %Δ Quantity
%Δ Price

Causes a math bias depending on starting price.

The Midpoint Method

(Q₂ - Q₁) / [(Q₂ + Q₁)/2]
÷
(P₂ - P₁) / [(P₂ + P₁)/2]

Uses the average to give us one true answer.

Speaker 1: Toki Tahmid

The Spectrum of Curves

Simple Explanation: Elasticity isn't just one number; it's a whole range. A perfectly straight vertical line means people must buy the exact same amount no matter what the price is (like life-saving medicine). A perfectly flat horizontal line means people will only pay one exact price, and not a penny more.

Perfectly Inelastic (E=0)

Vertical: Quantity stays exact regardless of price (e.g., Insulin).

Unit Elastic (E=1)

A 10% price shift yields exactly a 10% quantity shift.

Perfectly Elastic (E=∞)

Horizontal: Buyers accept only one specific market price.

Speaker 2: Alfa Akter Anika

Determinants of Demand Elasticity

Simple Explanation: Elasticity depends on how people think and shop. These four main factors decide if a customer can easily walk away when a store raises its price.
💊 vs 💎

Necessities vs Luxuries

People will pay higher prices for life-saving medicine (inelastic). But they will skip buying a luxury watch if it gets too expensive (elastic).

🧈

Availability of Substitutes

If there are many other options (like different butter brands), demand is elastic. If there are no options, it's inelastic.

🍦

Market Definition

Broad categories like 'Food' have no substitutes (inelastic). Narrow categories like 'Vanilla Ice Cream' have tons of substitutes (elastic).

⏱️

Time Horizon

Right away, consumers are stuck in their habits. Given enough time, they will find alternatives, making demand more elastic.

Part I & II Recap

We've seen that Price Elasticity of Demand is the ultimate guide for a business setting its prices.

If you sell a necessity with no substitutes (Inelastic), you control the market revenue and can safely raise prices. But if you sell a luxury good with lots of competition (Elastic), the customers have the power. Raising prices in that market will destroy your revenue.

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

Simple Explanation: Now let's look at the sellers. Supply elasticity measures how fast and easily a business can make more products when the market price goes up. A steep curve means the factory is stuck and can't produce more easily. A flat curve means they can rapidly ramp up production.
Speaker 4: Sayda Maysha Hasan

Supply & Time Horizons

Simple Explanation: Time is the biggest factor for sellers. Right away, a factory can't change much (fixed supply). With more time, they can run double shifts (short run) or even build totally new factories (long run), making them highly flexible.
Time Elapsed Since Price Increase: Momentary (Fixed Supply)

The Farming Paradox

Simple Explanation: Can good news be bad for an industry? People only need to eat so much food, making demand steep (inelastic). If a new hybrid seed lets farmers grow way more crops, supply increases. But because demand is steep, the price crashes fast, and farmers actually lose money!

History Case: Why OPEC Failed

Simple Explanation: In the 1970s, people couldn't quickly change their driving habits, so when OPEC cut oil supply, prices skyrocketed. But by the 1980s, people bought better cars and found new oil sources. When OPEC tried cutting supply again, prices barely moved because the market had adapted and become highly elastic.

Speaker 5: Afrin Mahmud

Tax Incidence & Elasticity

Simple Explanation: The government doesn't decide who really pays a tax; elasticity does. A tax acts as a wedge between buyers and sellers. The burden falls hardest on whoever is less flexible (inelastic) because they can't easily leave the market to avoid the extra cost.

Key Takeaways

  • ▪ Elasticity tells us exactly how much buyers and sellers react to market changes.
  • ▪ Demand is more elastic (flexible) for luxuries, longer timeframes, and things with substitutes.
  • ▪ You can safely raise prices to boost revenue only if your market is inelastic.
  • ▪ Supply elasticity depends on how much time has passed and a factory's flexibility.
  • ▪ The cost of a tax will always fall on the least flexible (inelastic) person in the market.
